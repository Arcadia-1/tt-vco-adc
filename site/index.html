1 of 6, following the signal

Current in, current back

Each input meets the feedback as a current. A 49.7 kΩ resistor brings the signal in; seven 1.04 MΩ resistors, switched by the output bits, pull it back out. Both are cut from the same poly strips, so sheet-resistance drift cancels out of the gain.

  • ±0.60 V differential full scale
  • 42 matched strips plus dummies per half

2 of 6

Five transistors hold the summing node

The input pair is 48/3 µm, laid out A A B B B B A A around a common centroid. The large gate area keeps flicker noise low: 22.9 µVrms referred to the input over 0.1 to 50 kHz.

  • 47.8 dB gain, 14.7 MHz GBW
  • 72° phase margin, 23 µA tail

3 of 6

2.43 pF remembers the error

Two MIM capacitors per half sit between metal 3 and metal 4, pulled apart here so you can see them. They integrate the difference between input and feedback. The ring integrates its own phase as well, so the loop has two integrators and shapes quantization noise to second order.

  • 2 × 24 × 25 µm per half
  • Unity gain at 1.32 MHz

4 of 6

A clamp that costs nothing until it is needed

Past 7.5 MHz the phase detector aliases, and the loop would lock itself near 8.5 MHz and stay there. Five transistors beside each OTA keep the control voltage low enough that the ring never passes 6.66 MHz.

  • 14 to 49 nA leakage in normal operation
  • 0.00 dB noise and distortion penalty

5 of 6

The quantizer is a ring oscillator

Seven current-starved inverters. The integrator sets how fast they run, and how far the edge travels in one clock period is the measurement. At mid-scale the loop holds both rings at a quarter of the clock, 2.50 MHz.

  • 8.48 MHz/V
  • 11.5 µW for both rings

6 of 6

Sample, XOR, count

Flip-flops sample all seven phases at 10 MHz. XOR with the previous sample marks every stage the edge crossed, and those bits drive the resistor DAC directly. Output buffers keep pad capacitance away from that feedback path.

  • 14 flip-flops and 7 XOR gates per half
  • Feedback glitch under 0.16 ns with 1 to 5 pF on the pins

Three details that keep it robust

Each half is an OTA-C integrator ahead of a seven-stage ring. Three details protect it on silicon: a low-noise input pair, a clamp against false lock, and buffers between the pads and the feedback path.

A quieter input pair

A 48/3 µm NMOS pair in a common-centroid layout over a 16/2 µm PMOS load keeps flicker noise low. Gain is 47.8 dB and phase margin stays at 72° over corners.

22.9 µVrms input noise

A clamp against false lock

Above three quarters of the clock rate, the XOR differentiator aliases backward and the loop latches. A two-stage active clamp with no static current caps the ring at 6.66 MHz and rings by 0.32 mV at most when it engages.

0.00 dB noise and HD3 penalty

Pads kept off the feedback path

Dedicated buf_4 buffers separate the 1 to 5 pF pad and mux load from the flip-flops and XOR gates that drive the DAC. Glitch charge and rise-fall asymmetry do not depend on what is connected outside.

0.10 to 0.16 ns glitch at 1 to 5 pF

Bringing it up on the bench

Clock at 10 MHz, hold rst_n low for 2 µs, release, wait 50 µs, then capture all 16 outputs on every rising edge. Both monitor pins should read about 2.50 MHz once the loop has settled.
